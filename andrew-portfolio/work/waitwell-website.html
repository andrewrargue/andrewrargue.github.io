Case study

WaitWell Website Redesign

When design directly drives revenue — redesigning a B2B SaaS acquisition surface to close the gap between platform quality and web presence.

Client

WaitWell

Role

UI/UX Design Lead

Year

2024

Team

Anna Didenko (Supporting Designer), Patricia Orozco (Art Director)

Overview

WaitWell is a queue management and booking platform used by healthcare clinics, government offices, universities, retailers, and veterinary practices across North America. Their existing website undersold a platform with genuine market traction — and with US expansion on the horizon, that gap needed to close.

The old site failed its audience in three distinct ways: the brand signalled startup rather than enterprise, the conversion architecture was flat with no guided path to a demo, and six distinct buyer personas all landed on one generic page where nobody felt specifically addressed.

Disciplines

Web Design Conversion B2B SaaS Brand Information Architecture Design Systems

Business objectives

  • Align visual brand with company positioning
  • Establish design standards for all digital surfaces
  • Improve credibility and competitive differentiation
  • Support US market expansion
  • Drive Talk to Sales conversions
  • Deliver brand guide for post-launch consistency

Process

A structured end-to-end process with client checkpoints throughout.

Discovery Competitive audit Wireframing with copywriter Logo refinement Style tiles Full UI design Project UI kit Collaborative QA Brand guide

Key decision — Visual direction

Three directions, one filter: which signals enterprise credibility?

Three distinct visual directions were explored before committing to UI. Direction 1 (light, minimal) created accessibility concerns for government and healthcare buyers. Direction 2 (dark, bold) read as more techy than enterprise — visually indistinguishable from general SaaS tools, with none of the institutional credibility government and healthcare buyers needed to see.

Direction 3 (gradient, ownable) won — distinctive palette, clock motif as brand anchor, industry grid as conversion architecture. The filter wasn't aesthetic preference. It was enterprise credibility.

The question wasn't "which looks best?" — it was "which one looks like a platform a hospital network or municipal government would trust with their queue infrastructure?"

UI design — Making the product visible

Illustrations, photography, and UI woven together so the product feels real before anyone signs up.

Each page wove product illustrations throughout — simplified graphic interpretations of WaitWell's features used as visual context alongside headlines and feature descriptions. Paired with photography of real people in real service environments, the product felt tangible and human rather than abstract.

Photography was treated deliberately: portraits colour-graded toward the cool end of WaitWell's palette, kept bright and approachable, and paired specifically with the illustration or UI element it was meant to support — so a photo of someone checking their phone sits alongside the actual confirmation screen they'd see.

Each page followed a varied but consistent rhythm — alternating background treatments and layouts (icon grids, image-and-text panels, dashboard mockups, testimonial cards) — all operating within the Tiller design system's spacing and grid rules. The variety kept long pages engaging, while consistent typography, button styles, and colour kept every page feeling unified.

Key decision — Token architecture

Two-tier token system: 80 primitives feeding 46 semantic tokens.

80 primitives (Indigo, Purple, Teal, Lime, Grey, White) feed into 46 semantic tokens across Surface/20, Button/7, Text/7, Illustration/11, and Border/1. Every component references semantic tokens, never raw values.

A rebrand or dark mode change touches the primitive layer and propagates automatically. The system was built for the next designer, not just this project.

UI design — Feature pages

Platform depth shown, not just described.

Feature pages needed to convey the breadth and sophistication of WaitWell's platform to buyers evaluating it against competitors. Each feature was shown in context — UI screenshots embedded within illustrated environments rather than floating in isolation.

The goal was to give a visitor enough confidence in the platform's depth that the demo request felt like the obvious next step, not a leap of faith.

UI design — Social proof

Trust signals built into the page structure, not added as an afterthought.

Client logos, testimonial cards, and case study callouts were treated as structural elements of each page rather than a single block to scroll past. Testimonials are visually formatted to surface the strongest claim — highlighted key phrases catch a scanning visitor's eye before they commit to reading the full quote.

For a platform selling to risk-averse institutional buyers, social proof isn't decoration — it's a core part of the conversion architecture.

Deliverable — Brand guide

Decisions made in the project, documented for every project after.

Every design decision made during the project — photography direction, illustration usage, card styles, form field treatments, colour application — was documented as part of WaitWell's brand guide at the end of the engagement.

The brand guide gives WaitWell's internal team and future design partners a shared reference point, so the standards built into the redesign don't erode as the site grows and evolves after launch.

Built for the next designer, not just this project.

Logo system, colour tokens, photography direction, and print collateral, documented for handoff to WaitWell's internal team and future design partners.
