Case study

One Exchange — Trading Platform Redesign

Rapid design iteration across four interconnected platforms for a multi-platform financial trading system.

Client

One Exchange

Role

Product Designer

Year

2026

Team

Patricia Orozco (Product Design, Art Direction)

Overview

One Exchange operates four interconnected platforms: OX Trading Platform (trader and broker views), OX CO2 Trading, and a Customer Portal. We focused deep design iteration on the OX Trading Platform as the flagship product.

The engagement required designing for high-information-density environments where traders make split-second decisions. We designed 16 flows across 79 screens, spanning trader views, broker views, and login.

Disciplines

Product Design FinTech Design Systems

Scope

  • OX Trading Platform — trader and broker views
  • OX CO2 Trading — carbon trading views
  • Customer Portal — layout, report views, table patterns
  • Cross-platform — login flows, settings, confirms, indices

Key challenge — Data density

Hierarchy traders can read at a glance under pressure.

Financial trading requires surfacing a large volume of data without creating cognitive overload. Every element on the trading desk competes for attention — the design system had to establish clear hierarchy that traders could read at a glance under pressure.

I deliberately desaturated the overall colour palette — not as a wholesale redesign, but as a series of careful, incremental changes that respected the mental models traders already had of the platform. Heavy, saturated colour blocking in the original design meant every element competed equally for attention. By letting structural chrome (headers, dividers, row striping) recede, the few remaining colour signals — bid and offer cells, hold states, pinned rows — could actually register as meaningful when a trader's eye landed on them.

I also clarified the navigation hierarchy with more intentional colour use: primary actions, utility toggles, and platform navigation became three visually distinct groups instead of one undifferentiated row of buttons.

Key challenge — Dual views

Two fundamentally different users. One coherent system.

The trader view is built for speed — placing and managing orders, monitoring live market data, and acting on position changes in real time. Every element is optimised for decision velocity. Screen space is dense by design.

The broker view serves a different purpose: oversight. Brokers monitor client activity across multiple accounts, manage the order book, and review the trade book. The information hierarchy shifts from action-first to visibility-first — same underlying data, different emphasis and layout logic.

Designing both views within one component system required establishing which patterns were shared and which needed to diverge — without fracturing the visual language between them.

Key challenge — Dialogue system

Order confirmations and modals required systematic thinking across dozens of edge cases.

Order confirmations and modals required systematic thinking across dozens of edge cases — every trade type, error state, and confirmation flow needed to follow the same underlying pattern without fragmenting into one-off solutions.

Key challenge — New order flow

The most iterated screen on the project — 37 versions to get it right.

The new order flow is the most critical interaction on the trading desk — the path from intent to placed trade. It needed to handle multiple order types, validation states, and confirmation steps without adding friction to what traders do dozens of times a day.

The New Order modal went through 37 iterations to reach a pattern that handled the full range of trade types, error states, and confirmation flows without fragmenting. The central decision being resolved: what level of selection control belongs to a tab, a radio button, and a dropdown — and how to make that hierarchy legible to a trader placing orders under pressure.

Early versions had too many competing tab styles, with no clear logic for when to use which. This evolved into three distinct levels of selection: tabs for primary trade type, radio buttons for sub-options, and dropdowns for detailed parameters — each level visually distinct so traders always knew what kind of choice they were making.

37 iterations across layout, field order, error handling, and confirmation treatment — each one testing whether the hierarchy held across a new trade type or edge case, not just refining aesthetics.

Key challenge — Order book

Live market data at a density traders can act on.

The order book surfaces bid and ask prices, volumes, and market depth in real time. At this density, every typographic and colour decision has a functional consequence — misread data means a bad trade.

Colour, weight, and spacing were calibrated to let traders parse price levels instantly, with visual differentiation between bid, ask, and spread that holds up under extended screen time.

Deliverable — UI kit

A handoff file the development team actually wanted to use.

The UI kit documents every component across the OX Trading Platform with a semantic variable system — colours, spacing, and type referenced by role, not raw value. Happy-path flows organised specifically for engineering reference, reducing the interpretation overhead that typically adds time to implementation.

The handoff received positive feedback from the development team — a signal that the organisation and documentation matched how they actually build.

Good handoff means fewer Slack messages after delivery.

Results

The 16 flows and 79 screens reflect the scope of a genuinely complex system — four interconnected platforms, two fundamentally different user types, and dozens of edge cases that each needed a resolved pattern rather than a one-off solution. The volume is a consequence of the problem, not the goal.
